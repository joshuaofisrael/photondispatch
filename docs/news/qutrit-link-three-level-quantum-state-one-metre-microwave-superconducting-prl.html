Physicists sent a three-level quantum state between two chips, something a qubit link can’t carry

By PhotonDispatch Staff

Published 20 November 2026 · Last updated 20 November 2026

Most quantum links move qubits, which use two energy levels. A team has now moved three. "A three-level quantum state crossed a one-meter microwave link between separate superconducting chips, extending quantum information transfer beyond the two levels used by qubits," Phys.org posted on X on Wednesday night, highlighting a paper in Physical Review Letters.

## What they built

The preprint on arXiv is from researchers at the Institute of Physics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and partner institutions. It describes two independently packaged nodes joined by a microwave channel. Each node has a transmon qutrit, a resonator and a tunable filter so the two ends can be matched in frequency and bandwidth. The two upper levels of the qutrit are sent in separate time windows down the same channel.

The team reports a mean transferred-state fidelity of 83.68% and a process fidelity of 77.12%. According to the abstract, both beat the classical benchmark for qutrit transfer and the best a qubit channel could manage when carrying a qutrit.

## Why three levels

A qutrit has three basis states rather than two, so each one can carry more information. The catch, as QubitWire puts it, is that the receiver has to preserve the relationships among all three components, which makes the transfer harder.

The paper also reports a remote two-qutrit entangled state and a dense-coding capacity of 2.273 bits, above the qubit limit. QubitWire notes that this figure, and a Bell-type parameter, are inferred from tomography rather than measured in a direct communication test. The paper does not claim a loophole-free Bell test.

## What it means

This is a lab link over one metre, not a network. It shows that the extra energy levels already present in transmon qubits can be used as a real channel between modules. Whether that saves hardware in a modular quantum computer depends on the cost of preparing, calibrating and correcting those extra levels, a comparison the paper leaves open. Our coverage of [IBM's modular co-design work](/news/ibm-nvidia-fermilab-modular-quantum-co-design-platform-sc26.html) looks at why links between modules matter.

The paper was published in Physical Review Letters on 28 October.

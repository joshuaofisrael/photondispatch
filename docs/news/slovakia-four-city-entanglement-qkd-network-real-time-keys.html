Slovak researchers report a four-city entanglement QKD network delivering keys in real time over existing fibre

By PhotonDispatch Staff

Published 15 November 2026 · Last updated 15 November 2026

Short answer: A team working on Slovakia's national quantum communication infrastructure has reported an entanglement-based quantum key distribution (QKD) network that links four cities in the west of the country over deployed telecom fibre and delivers finished keys in real time. The work was posted to arXiv on 12 November.

According to Quantum.world, the network connects Bratislava, Trnava, Nitra and Trenčín. A central node in Trnava generates polarisation-entangled photon pairs and routes them by wavelength, so Trnava, Bratislava and Nitra form a fully meshed sub-network without trusted relay nodes, while Trenčín connects through the central node. The paper says all links ran at the same time and keys are handed to applications through the ETSI GS QKD 014 interface.

The authors stress that their rates are measured after the complete post-processing chain rather than estimated from raw photon coincidences. With partial dispersion compensation they report 13.5 bit/s over 109 km, and 4.3 bit/s over a link with 32.1 dB of accumulated loss between two cities that have no direct fibre between them.

The paper is candid about a security gap. When the team benchmarked its privacy amplification against finite-key bounds, the heuristic setting used in the deployment could produce more key than the bound allows, and on the 142.9 km link the implemented scheme yields no certifiable key at all. The authors estimate that replacing their detect-and-discard reconciliation with true error correction would raise the finite-key rate by a factor of 4.1 to 6.6 and give a positive rate on that longest link.

The preprint also introduces a matrix formalism for planning passive wavelength routing so that every pair of users shares exactly one entangled channel.

Note: The results are from a preprint that has not yet been peer reviewed. The authors report that the key on the longest link is not certified under their finite-key bound.
